# Guy Moretti Background and Commentary Dossier

Status: fictional canon established; historical framing researched; no lines
approved for recording

## Canon summary

- Guido “Guy” Moretti is 21, 5'10", and 190 pounds.
- He grew up in New York City and was a champion high-school wrestler.
- He moved to Chicago after school, lives near Taylor Street, and remains
  truthfully billed from New York City.
- He worked for Raffin Construction Company, first tending a mason and later
  laying brick.
- About one year before the campaign, he took a cash challenge at a traveling
  carnival and legitimately defeated the attraction's sitting champion.
- A Chicago promoter investigated the story, learned of Guy's legitimate
  wrestling background, and invited him to train for professional work.
- Guy left full-time masonry and worked a few dozen small professional bouts
  before receiving his first sustained opportunity at the Marigold.
- For Saturday Marigold cards in or after May 1952, he takes the Route 9
  Ashland bus north, gets off near Grace Street, and walks east to 817 West
  Grace.

Guy, his employment, the carnival incident, the promoter, and the trip are
fictional. Real names and historical details ground the story but are not
evidence that the fictional events occurred.

## Historical grounding

### Taylor Street

Chicago's Little Italy developed on the Near West Side around Halsted and
Taylor Streets near Hull House. It provides a specific, credible Italian
neighborhood for Guy without changing his New York origins.

The game may describe Guy's own rented room and circumstances as poor or
crowded. It should describe the district itself as **working-class Taylor
Street** rather than reducing a real, varied community to “the poor part of
Chicago.”

Status: `verified` for general neighborhood context.

Source:

- [Little Italy — Encyclopedia of Chicago](https://www.encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/758.html)

### Raffin Construction Company

Louis Raffin, a mason from northern Italy, immigrated to Chicago in 1912. He
helped found Kensington Concrete Construction Company in 1916, bought out his
partners, and renamed the business Raffin Construction Company in 1922. The
company's documented early work included interior masonry at Rockefeller
Chapel from 1925 through 1928, and its history records that it survived the
Depression and war years.

This makes Raffin an unusually good historical fit: it was a real, established
Chicago masonry contractor, founded by an Italian immigrant mason, and active
through Guy's working years. Guy's place on one of its crews is deliberate
fiction. Never phrase it as a discovered company record or attribute opinions,
conduct, or workplace events to real Raffin personnel.

Status: `verified company history`; `fictional employment`.

Source:

- [The Raffin Legacy — Raffin Construction](https://raffinconstruction.com/why-raffin/legacy)

### The Ashland ride

Historical route summaries identify Ashland as Route 9 and date weekend bus
replacement of its streetcars to May 11, 1952; complete conversion followed
in February 1954. Because Marigold wrestling moved to Saturdays in January
1952, a Saturday story card after May 11 can credibly place Guy on the Ashland
bus. Earlier or weekday scenes require date-specific checking.

This is a useful visual journey, not an exact door-to-door route: Guy must get
from Taylor Street west to Ashland, ride north, then walk east from Ashland
toward the arena at 817 West Grace.

Status: `provisional`; confirm the 1952 service detail against the cited
historical transit source before recording an exact route line.

Sources:

- [Ashland Avenue route history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashland_Avenue)
- Alan R. Lind, *Chicago Surface Lines: An Illustrated History* (1974), cited
  by the route history
- [1952 Chicago wrestling chronology](https://www.legacyofwrestling.com/Chicago52.html)

### Carnival challenge

Audience challenges, cash prizes, and house champions belong to wrestling's
carnival and athletic-show history. By the early 1950s this route into the
business should feel residual rather than ordinary: Guy catches one of the old
shows late in its life, which is why the incident becomes a story worth
repeating.

The specific carnival, bet, house champion, outcome, and discovery are
fictional. Do not invent a real carnival company and then imply it staged the
incident.

Status: `historically plausible fictional incident`; a stronger scholarly
source is still needed before explanatory commentary is finalized.

## Draft commentary lines

These are tonal prototypes, not recording-ready copy.

- “There is the old school wrestler in Moretti—a clean turn, and no wasted
  motion.”
- “Those hands laid brick in Chicago not a year ago. Once Moretti has hold of
  something, he is accustomed to keeping it where it belongs.”
- “He carried the hod before they trusted him with the wall. This young man
  knows something about working while tired.”
- “The story around Taylor Street is that Moretti entered a carnival tent on a
  wager and came out after beating the champion. Tonight's wager is his place
  in this business.”
- “He has had a few dozen professional contests, but very few against a man who
  can disguise his intentions like this one.”
- “Moretti rode north on the Ashland bus this evening. A victory here may not
  buy an automobile, but it could put his name on next Saturday's bill.”
- “A year ago he was laying a straight course of brick. Tonight he must find a
  straight course out of that corner.”

## Writing limits

- During ordinary play, allow no more than one new biographical fact per match.
- Do not repeatedly equate manual labor with invulnerability. Guy can be tired,
  hurt, and frightened.
- Do not use a comedy Italian accent or turn Taylor Street into ethnic set
  dressing.
- Prefer “bout,” “match,” or “contest” in announcer copy. Guy may call one a
  “fight” in private dialogue.
- The carnival victory proves legitimate ability, not instant professional
  mastery.
- The New York school championship remains general until a historically
  credible school and competition structure are verified.

## Open research

- Select Guy's New York neighborhood, school, graduation year, and the exact
  scope of his high-school title.
- Establish where Raffin Construction was based and what kind of crew and job
  would plausibly employ a young bricklayer in 1949–51.
- Confirm Route 9's May 1952 weekend conversion from a primary or dedicated
  transit-history source and choose Guy's likely boarding and alighting stops.
- Research surviving Chicago-area carnival or athletic-show activity in
  1950–51 before deciding whether the incident needs a place and date.
- Decide the Chicago promoter's identity. A fictional intermediary offers more
  story freedom; a real promoter requires a time-locked biographical dossier.
